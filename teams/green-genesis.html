Team Deep Dive · AgriTech

Green Genesis

A farm-to-consumer marketplace for verified organic produce, with QR-code traceability from farm to plate.

Mentor: Mr. Rakesh Potential CoI (Raj) — not used as a deck example

What they submitted

Problem
Consumers can't verify that food labeled organic is genuinely chemical-free; farmers growing natural produce lack direct access to customers and lose margin to middlemen. The organic market lacks trust, transparency, and fair pricing.
Target customers
Health-conscious families · fitness enthusiasts · parents · restaurants, hotels, and cafés · farmers seeking direct access and fair prices
Solution
A digital platform connecting verified organic farmers directly with consumers. QR-code traceability lets customers verify farm origin and quality; farmers receive fairer prices.

Read: a well-stated problem — trust really is the organic market's core failure. But the submission stacks three hard businesses into one: a certification/verification service, a two-sided marketplace, and perishable-goods logistics. The word doing all the work is "verified." The grilling should pull on that thread first, then the marketplace mechanics.

1The verification claim

Trust is the product. A QR code proves a database entry exists, not that the farm is chemical-free. What actually backs the promise?

  • "Verified" by whom, how? Walk us through verifying one farm: who visits, what do they check, how often?
  • India already has organic certification — NPOP third-party certification and PGS-India peer guarantee, plus FSSAI's Jaivik Bharat label for retail. Are you piggybacking on those, or replacing them? If replacing: why would anyone trust you more?
  • Does verification include lab testing for pesticide residue? What does one test cost, per batch or per farm, and who pays?
  • What happens when a verified farmer cheats once — sprays during a bad pest season? How would you even find out?
  • The QR code shows what the farmer entered. What stops garbage-in, garbage-out?

2Marketplace cold start

Two-sided marketplaces die at the start: farmers won't join without buyers, buyers won't come without supply.

  • Which side do you build first, and in which one city or even one neighborhood?
  • How many verified farmers do you need for a credible week-one catalog? How many do you have relationships with today?
  • Their target list is families, fitness enthusiasts, parents, and HoReCa. That's everyone. Pick one. Which segment feels the trust pain most and pays fastest? (Hint: restaurants buy weekly and in volume, but demand consistency; families forgive more but order small.)

3Perishability & logistics

Fresh produce delivery has buried well-funded startups. The vegetables don't wait for the app to scale.

  • Who moves the produce — you, the farmer, or a 3PL? Same-day? Cold chain?
  • What is your expected wastage percentage, and who eats that cost?
  • Unit economics of one order: average order value, delivery cost, platform margin. Does a ₹400 vegetable basket survive a ₹80 delivery?
  • Is a weekly subscription box (predictable demand, batched delivery) a better first product than an on-demand marketplace?

4Competition & today's alternative

Today's alternative is rarely a startup. Here it's the trusted local vendor, a farmers' market, or a WhatsApp group run by a farmer.

  • Organic farm-to-consumer has been tried repeatedly in India — subscription farm boxes, community-supported agriculture, organic aisles at BigBasket and every supermarket. Why did the failures fail, and what's different about you?
  • A health-conscious family already has a source they half-trust. What makes them switch — and pay more?
  • How much premium over regular produce will your customer actually pay: 20%? 50%? 100%? Have you asked ten of them?

5Disintermediation — the marketplace leak

The classic failure: the platform makes the introduction, then the two sides cut it out.

  • After the first delivery, the customer has the farmer's number. Why does order #2 go through you?
  • What is your take rate — and after your cut plus logistics, is the farmer's price actually "fairer" than the mandi price? Show the math for one crate of tomatoes.
  • What do you provide that survives the relationship forming: the trust mark? aggregated demand? payments and disputes? Name the thing.

6Scoping & MVP

The riskiest assumption is behavioral, not technical: will consumers pay a premium for verification they can see? That's testable with almost no code.

  • What's the smallest slice? For example: 10 farmers, one neighborhood, a WhatsApp group, and a weekly box — with you personally doing the farm visits and deliveries. Concierge first, platform later.
  • What can you learn in two weeks with under ₹5,000? (Stand outside one organic store, offer a "farm-verified" box pre-order, count who pays.)
  • Which do you test first: consumers paying the premium, or farmers accepting your terms? What's the pass mark, committed in advance?

7Team & resources

  • Does anyone on the team know a farmer? How many farm visits have you made since submitting this idea?
  • Who does farm verification credibly — do you have an agronomist, or access to SRM's agriculture department?
  • Verification visits, lab tests, and logistics all cost money before revenue. What does the first month cost, and who funds it?
The kill-shot question

"After your take rate and delivery cost, show us the math where the farmer earns more and the family pays a price they accept — for one crate of tomatoes."

"Fair to farmers AND cheap for consumers" is the claim every middleman-removal pitch makes. The spread has to fund the entire company.

Facts for the mentor table

  • India's organic framework: NPOP (third-party certification, APEDA) and PGS-India (peer-guarantee for smallholders); FSSAI's Jaivik Bharat logo governs retail organic labeling.
  • Certification is costly and slow for smallholders — which is exactly why many genuine natural farmers are uncertified, and why "verified" is both the opportunity and the hard part.
  • QR traceability is only as trustworthy as the data source; without inspection or testing behind it, it's marketing.
  • Fresh-produce e-commerce margins are thin and wastage is real; subscription boxes with batched delivery are the survivable entry pattern.
  • Disintermediation is the standard failure mode of direct farm-to-consumer marketplaces.

Grilling context, not gospel — have the students bring current certification costs and real price spreads. That's the point.
